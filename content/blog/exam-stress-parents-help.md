Some nerves before exams are normal, and a little pressure can even help a child focus. Stress becomes a problem when it takes over sleep, eating, mood or confidence. The NHS notes that children often feel the most pressure at exam time from their own family, which means parents have a big and helpful role to play. This guide covers the signs to look for, what to do at home, and when to ask for support.

## Why exam time feels so heavy in India

Board exams, entrance tests, tuition schedules and comparison with cousins and classmates can make marks feel like the whole story. Children often pick up our worry even when we say nothing. A child who hears "this year decides your future" is carrying a very large weight for their age. Naming that weight, and taking some of it off, is the first step.

## Signs of exam stress to look for

Stress shows up differently in each child. The NHS and other health services list changes such as:

- Eating much less or much more than usual
- Trouble falling asleep, or waking very early
- Headaches, stomach aches or feeling tired with no clear medical cause
- Being irritable, tearful or more withdrawn
- Dropping hobbies or friends they usually enjoy
- Negative talk such as "I'll fail anyway" or feeling hopeless about the future

One or two of these for a few days around a test is common. A pattern that lasts is worth taking seriously.

## What helps at home

### 1. Listen before you advise

Ask "How are you feeling about the exams?" and then let your child talk. The NHS advises parents to listen, offer support and avoid criticism. You do not have to fix anything. "That sounds stressful, and I am on your side" is often enough.

### 2. Protect sleep

Late-night cramming usually costs more than it gives. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 9 to 12 hours of sleep for children aged 6 to 12, and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers aged 13 to 18. Tired brains learn and remember less and feel stress more strongly. Keep a steady bedtime, switch screens off well before sleep, and avoid last-night marathons. Our guide on [how much sleep children need](/blog/how-much-sleep-children-need) has practical tips.

### 3. Keep meals regular and simple

Regular meals help children concentrate. Offer familiar, home-cooked food: dal, roti or rice, vegetables, curd, eggs if you eat them, fruit, a handful of nuts for older children. Water within reach helps too. See our post on a [balanced plate for Indian kids](/blog/balanced-plate-indian-kids). Try not to let sweet drinks and packaged snacks replace meals during study time.

### 4. Make room for movement and breaks

The World Health Organization advises an average of at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous physical activity a day for children and adolescents aged 5 to 17. A walk, cycling, badminton or dancing at home all count, and exercise helps the body settle stress. Short breaks between study blocks, with a stretch, some water and a few slow breaths, are better than hours of unbroken sitting. See [how much exercise kids need](/blog/physical-activity-children-need).

### 5. Plan the study together, in small steps

A big syllabus feels scary. Sit with your child and break it into small daily chunks, with a rest day each week and time for something they enjoy. A written plan on the wall can quiet the "I haven't done enough" feeling. Let your child have a say in it.

### 6. Watch the words you use

Compare these:

| Pressure | Support |
| --- | --- |
| "Everyone else is scoring higher." | "I can see how hard you are working." |
| "Your whole future depends on this." | "This exam matters, and it is not the measure of you." |
| "Why can't you focus?" | "What would make studying feel easier today?" |

Praise effort and steady habits more than marks. Marks are one result; the way your child handles a hard time is a skill they will use all their life.

### 7. Look after your own calm

Children borrow our calm. If you are anxious, take a few slow breaths, talk to another adult, and keep the house routine steady. Agree with grandparents and relatives that everyone will ask fewer questions about marks in the weeks before exams.

## On exam day and after

Keep the morning calm and the breakfast easy. Skip last-minute quizzing. After the exam, ask "How are you feeling?" before "How did it go?" and avoid going through every answer. If results disappoint, begin with comfort and a plan, not blame. Many children feel ashamed even before you say a word.

## When to seek help

Reach out to a school counsellor, your paediatrician or a child psychologist if your child:

- Has been very anxious, low or irritable for more than two weeks
- Sleeps or eats very little for days, or is often unwell with no medical cause
- Has panic attacks, such as racing heart, breathlessness or an overwhelming sense of dread
- Avoids school or refuses to sit exams
- Says they are hopeless, a failure or a burden

If your child ever talks about hurting themselves or not wanting to live, take it seriously and seek help the same day. Support is available for families at Modern Psych Therapy, and asking early is a sign of care.

## The takeaway

Exams are a small part of a long life, and your child needs to know you see them as more than their marks. Listen, protect sleep, offer simple meals and movement, plan in small steps, and choose your words with care. If stress is lasting or growing, getting support early helps the whole family.
