There is no single magic number for children's screen time, but there are clear guidelines for the early years, and a simple principle for older children: screens should not crowd out sleep, play, movement, meals and time with family. This guide sets out what the World Health Organization (WHO) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend by age, and how to make it work in a real Indian household with school, homework and busy schedules.

## What WHO recommends for children under 5

In 2019 the WHO published guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under five. They treat screen time as a kind of sedentary time, and say:

| Age | WHO guidance on sedentary screen time |
| --- | --- |
| Under 1 year | None |
| 1 to 2 years | None recommended; if any, no more than 1 hour a day, and less is better |
| 3 to 4 years | No more than 1 hour a day; less is better |

The WHO also says that when children are sedentary, reading and storytelling with a caregiver is a better use of that time. The reasoning is that young children learn best through movement, play and conversation with real people, and that time on a screen replaces those things.

## What AAP recommends

The AAP's long-standing advice for younger children is similar:

- **Under 18 months:** avoid screen media, apart from video-chatting with family, for example with grandparents in another city.
- **18 to 24 months:** if you want to introduce screens, choose high-quality programmes and watch them together with your child.
- **2 to 5 years:** limit non-educational screen use to about 1 hour a day on weekdays, choose high-quality content, and watch together when you can.
- **6 years and older:** there is no fixed number. The AAP asks parents to set consistent limits so that media does not take the place of enough sleep, physical activity and other healthy behaviours, and to keep some places and times screen-free, such as mealtimes and bedrooms.

The AAP also offers a Family Media Use Plan, a short written agreement that families can build together. It is a useful way to turn general advice into rules that fit your home.

## What matters as much as the minutes

Total time is only part of the picture. The AAP and WHO both point to these questions:

- **What is your child watching or doing?** A video call with a grandparent, a creative drawing app, and endless autoplay videos are very different experiences.
- **Is anyone watching with them?** Talking about what is on the screen helps young children understand it.
- **What is being pushed out?** If screens are replacing sleep, outdoor play, homework, reading or meals, that is the signal to adjust.
- **How does your child feel afterwards?** Calm and curious is a good sign. Irritable, wired or tearful when it ends deserves a closer look at the routine.
- **What are the adults doing?** Children copy what they see. A phone at the dinner table sends a stronger message than any rule.

## Screens and sleep

Sleep is where screens most often cause trouble. The AAP advises turning off screens at least an hour before bed and keeping devices out of children's bedrooms overnight. Bright light and exciting content can make it harder to wind down. ## Practical ways to set limits without daily battles

1. **Decide the rules in advance, in calm moments.** Arguments in the middle of a favourite show rarely go well. Agree the times and places first.
2. **Make screen-free zones.** Meals, the bedroom and the hour before bed are the easiest to defend, and the ones that matter most.
3. **Give a warning.** "Five more minutes, then we switch off" is kinder and works better than a sudden stop.
4. **Offer something to move to.** Switching off is easier when there is a snack, a game, a walk or a story waiting.
5. **Use screens as a tool, not a reward or a punishment.** If screens become the prize for eating dinner or finishing homework, they become more valuable in your child's mind.
6. **Plan for school days and holidays.** Rules that suit term time can be relaxed a little in holidays, as long as sleep, outdoor play and meals stay protected.
7. **Involve the whole household.** Grandparents, cousins and caregivers need to be on the same page, and so do you.

Children from age six or seven can help write the family plan. Teenagers will resist rules they had no part in, but they often accept limits they helped design.

## When it is not just "too much screen time"

Sometimes heavy screen use is a symptom rather than the cause. A child may be using a device to avoid worry, loneliness, bullying or a hard day at school. Looking only at the hours can miss that.

## When to seek help

Consider talking to a psychologist or your child's doctor if you notice:

- Intense distress, anger or meltdowns that are hard to settle whenever screens are taken away
- Screens regularly replacing sleep, meals, school or friends
- Your child withdrawing from activities and people they used to enjoy
- Low mood, constant worry, or comments such as "no one likes me"
- Trouble paying attention or following conversations that has become a concern for the family or the school
- Your own attempts to set limits have repeatedly led to major conflict at home

Support is available, and you do not need to wait for a crisis. A first conversation can help you understand what is behind the behaviour and plan a calm way forward.

## The takeaway

For children under five, the WHO and AAP advise very little screen time, with none in the first year and no more than about an hour a day with an adult nearby after that. For older children, protect sleep, movement, play and family time first, and fit screens around them. Choose good content, watch together when you can, and model the habits you want to see. Small, consistent routines work better than strict rules that change every day.
