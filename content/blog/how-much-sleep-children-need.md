Most school-age children need **9 to 12 hours of sleep a night**, and teenagers need **8 to 10**. Many get far less. Sleep is not wasted time for a growing child: it is when the brain stores what was learned during the day, settles emotions, and the body grows and repairs. This guide covers how much sleep children need at each age, the signs that they are short of it, and simple steps that make bedtime easier for the whole family.

## How much sleep children need at each age

These ranges come from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and are endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics. They include naps for younger children.

| Age | Recommended sleep in 24 hours |
| --- | --- |
| 4–12 months | 12–16 hours |
| 1–2 years | 11–14 hours |
| 3–5 years | 10–13 hours |
| 6–12 years | 9–12 hours |
| 13–18 years | 8–10 hours |

Every child is a little different. Look at the range, then at your child: a child who wakes fairly easily, stays in a steady mood through the day and doesn't fall asleep in the car every afternoon is probably getting enough.

## Why sleep matters so much for children

### Learning and memory

During deep sleep the brain sorts through the day and turns new information into lasting memory. A child who sleeps well after a day of learning remembers more of it. That is one reason late-night studying before an exam often backfires.

### Emotions and behaviour

Tired children rarely look sleepy. More often they are irritable, quick to cry, restless or "hyper". Short sleep makes it harder to manage frustration and to focus, and some children who seem inattentive in class are simply exhausted.

### Growth and health

Growth hormone is released mainly during deep sleep. Regular, sufficient sleep also supports the immune system and is linked with a healthier weight.

## Signs your child may not be getting enough sleep

- Difficulty waking up on most school mornings
- Falling asleep during short car or bus rides
- Frequent meltdowns, irritability or tearfulness, especially in the late afternoon
- Trouble concentrating, or teachers mentioning daydreaming or restlessness
- Sleeping much longer on weekends and holidays to "catch up"

## A calm bedtime routine that helps

A predictable routine tells the body it is time to wind down. Keep it simple and do it in the same order each night.

1. **Fix a bedtime and a wake-up time**, including on weekends where you can. Keeping weekends within an hour of school days is a good target.
2. **Switch off screens an hour before bed.** The light from phones and tablets delays the body's sleep signal, and exciting videos keep the mind busy.
3. **Make the hour before bed quiet.** A warm bath, changing into night clothes, brushing teeth, then a story or quiet talk.
4. **Keep the room dark, cool and calm.** A fan or white noise can mask household sounds, which is helpful in joint families where adults stay up later.
5. **Watch late meals and drinks.** A heavy dinner right before bed, or tea, coffee and cola in the evening, can make sleep restless. A light, early dinner works best.
6. **End the day with connection.** A few minutes of talking about the day, or something the child is grateful for, helps anxious children settle.

## Common sleep challenges

**"Just five more minutes."** Give a 10-minute warning before the routine starts, and let your child choose small things (which story, which pyjamas) so bedtime feels less like a battle.

**Night-time fears.** Take the fear seriously without making it bigger. A night light, a comfort object and a calm, brief check-in work better than long conversations at midnight.

**Teenagers who can't fall asleep early.** Teens' body clocks naturally shift later, so they are not simply being stubborn. Protecting the last hour from screens and keeping wake-up times regular helps more than an early bedtime alone.

**Exam season.** Children often trade sleep for study. Remind them, and yourself, that a rested brain recalls more than a tired one.

## When to seek help

Talk to a paediatrician or a child psychologist if your child:

- Snores loudly most nights, or seems to pause breathing during sleep
- Regularly takes more than 30–45 minutes to fall asleep despite a good routine
- Wakes often through the night for weeks at a time
- Is very sleepy during the day even after enough hours in bed
- Has sleep problems along with ongoing worry, low mood or behaviour changes

Sleep difficulties are often linked with anxiety, stress or developmental differences, and they usually improve with the right support.

## The takeaway

Children need more sleep than most of us think: around 9 to 12 hours for school-age children and 8 to 10 for teenagers. A regular schedule, screen-free wind-down time and a calm, connected bedtime make a real difference to mood, learning and health. If sleep stays difficult, you don't have to work it out alone.
