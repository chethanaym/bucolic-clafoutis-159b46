A balanced plate for a child does not need to be fancy. The Indian Council of Medical Research and the National Institute of Nutrition (ICMR-NIN) describe it in their Dietary Guidelines for Indians as a mix of foods from several groups, built mostly from everyday home food: a cereal or millet, a pulse or other protein, vegetables, some fruit, and milk or curd. This guide shows what that looks like on a real plate, and how to get there without a daily battle.

## What ICMR-NIN means by a balanced diet

The guidelines use the idea of "My Plate for the Day": instead of counting nutrients, you look at whether the day's food covers the main groups. No single food does everything, so variety is the point.

| Food group | Everyday Indian examples |
| --- | --- |
| Cereals and millets | Roti, rice, ragi, jowar, bajra, poha, upma |
| Pulses, eggs and other protein | Dal, rajma, chana, sprouts, eggs, fish or chicken for families who eat them |
| Vegetables | Leafy greens, beans, gourds, carrots, tomatoes |
| Fruit | Banana, guava, papaya, orange, seasonal local fruit |
| Milk and milk products | Milk, curd, buttermilk, paneer |
| Nuts and seeds | Groundnuts, til, flax, almonds in small amounts |
| Fats and oils | A modest amount of the cooking oil or ghee you already use |

The guidelines also encourage eating a wide range of foods across the week, rather than the same few items, and a mix of colours of vegetables and fruit.

## A simple way to build the plate

You do not have to weigh anything. A rough picture is enough:

- **Roughly half the plate from the staple and protein.** Roti or rice with a generous helping of dal, sprouts, egg or another protein. ICMR-NIN advises that cereals should not crowd out everything else, and that pulses and other protein foods matter alongside them.
- **A good share of vegetables and fruit.** Add a vegetable to every meal, and offer fruit as a snack or dessert.
- **Milk or curd once or twice a day.** For many Indian children, this is a main source of calcium.
- **A small handful of nuts or seeds** where age and allergies allow. Whole nuts can be a choking risk for young children, so crush or powder them for under-fives.

Mixing is fine. Vegetable parathas, dal khichdi with a spoon of ghee, ragi dosa with chutney, or idli with sambar all combine several groups in a single dish.

## Where children's plates often fall short

Surveys of Indian children and teenagers regularly find too much refined cereal and packaged snack food, and too little protein, fruit and vegetables. Nothing here is a parenting failure. Busy mornings, tiffin boxes and fussy phases make the easy option the common one.

Two points from the guidelines are worth keeping in mind:

- **Limit sugar, salt and ultra-processed snacks.** ICMR-NIN advises cutting back on foods high in sugar, salt and fat, such as chips, packaged biscuits and sweetened drinks. WHO also recommends keeping free sugars (added sugar, plus sugar in juices and honey) below 10% of daily energy for everyone, and says less than 5% gives extra benefit.
- **Water first.** Plain water, buttermilk or nimbu pani without much sugar beat sweet drinks for everyday thirst.

## Getting children to actually eat it

Knowing what is balanced is the easy part. These ideas make it more likely to happen:

1. **Serve small portions of new foods next to a food they like.** Children often need many exposures before they accept a new taste, so keep offering without pressure.
2. **Eat together when you can.** Children copy what they see. A family meal, even a few times a week, is a calm moment to connect as well as to eat.
3. **Let them help.** Washing greens, rolling a chapati or choosing a fruit at the market makes children more curious about what is on the plate.
4. **Hide nothing, but blend well.** Grated carrot in a paratha or spinach in dal is fine. Just also keep offering vegetables openly, so they learn what vegetables look and taste like.
5. **Avoid food as a reward or punishment.** "Finish your dal and you can have a sweet" can make sweets seem special and dal seem like a chore.
6. **Keep meals predictable.** Regular meal and snack times mean children arrive hungry but not desperate, and graze less between meals. Good sleep helps too; tired children tend to crave sugary food. Our guide on [how much sleep children need](/blog/how-much-sleep-children-need) has age-by-age ranges.

## Vegetarian and vegan families

Many Indian families are vegetarian, and ICMR-NIN's guidelines show that a well-planned vegetarian diet can meet children's needs, with dal, pulses, milk products and nuts as the main protein sources. Combining cereals and pulses (as in khichdi or dal-rice) is a traditional and sensible pairing. Families who avoid all animal foods should speak to a doctor or dietitian, because some nutrients, such as vitamin B12, are hard to get without planning.

## When to seek help

Most feeding worries pass with time and patience. Speak to your paediatrician, a dietitian or a child psychologist if your child:

- Eats a very narrow range of foods and strongly refuses new ones strongly
- Is losing weight, not gaining or growing as expected, or seems unusually tired
- Gags, chokes or becomes very distressed around certain textures or smells
- Has mealtimes that regularly end in tears or panic for the child or the family
- Shows intense worry about weight, shape or eating, skips meals, or hides food (more common in older children and teens)

## The takeaway

A balanced plate for an Indian child is a familiar one: roti or rice, dal or another protein, vegetables, fruit, and milk or curd, with sugary and packaged snacks kept as occasional extras. Aim for variety across the week rather than a perfect plate every meal, keep offering new foods without pressure, and make mealtimes calm. Small, steady habits matter far more than any single day.
