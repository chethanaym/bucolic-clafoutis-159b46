Most children eat more sugar than is good for them, and much of it comes from drinks, packaged snacks and sweets rather than from the sugar bowl. The World Health Organization (WHO) advises that "free sugars" should make up less than 10% of daily energy, and less than 5% for extra benefit. This guide explains what that means for a child in practice, where sugar hides, and how to cut back without turning food into a fight.

## What are "free sugars"?

Free sugars are sugars added to food and drink, plus the sugars naturally present in honey, jaggery, syrups, fruit juices and juice concentrates. The sugar inside a whole fruit or in plain milk is not counted, because it comes packaged with fibre and other nutrients. So a banana or a piece of papaya is a different thing from a glass of fruit juice, even though both taste sweet.

## How much sugar can children have?

The NHS in the UK gives daily upper limits for free sugars by age:

| Age | Free sugars, at most |
| --- | --- |
| 4 to 6 years | about 19 g (5 teaspoons) |
| 7 to 10 years | about 24 g (6 teaspoons) |
| 11 years and over | about 30 g (7 teaspoons) |

The American Heart Association, with support from the American Academy of Pediatrics, advises that children under 2 years should have no added sugar at all, and that children aged 2 to 18 should have less than about 25 g (6 teaspoons) a day. These are limits, not targets. Less is fine, and there is no nutritional need for added sugar.

## Where sugar hides

A single serving of some everyday foods can use up a large part of a child's daily limit:

- **Sweet drinks.** Fizzy drinks, packaged fruit juices, flavoured milk, energy drinks and sweetened malted drinks are the biggest sources for many children.
- **Breakfast cereals and flavoured yoghurts.** Read the label for "sugar" and look at the grams per serving.
- **Biscuits, cakes, chocolates and ice cream.** Easy to overlook as "small treats" when they happen daily.
- **Traditional sweets.** Laddoos, barfi, halwa and jalebi are loved and part of our festivals, and they are sugar (or jaggery) too. Jaggery and honey are still free sugars, so swapping one for the other does not remove the sugar.
- **Sauces and ready-to-eat foods.** Ketchup, sweet chutneys and instant mixes can contain more sugar than you expect.

## Why it matters

The WHO's guideline is based on evidence that eating less free sugar lowers the risk of tooth decay and unhealthy weight gain. Sweet foods also tend to be filling without being nourishing, so a child who has had biscuits and juice may have less appetite for dal, vegetables, roti and curd. The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR-NIN) also advises families to limit sugary and highly processed foods and to build meals around a variety of whole foods; see our guide to a [balanced plate for Indian kids](/blog/balanced-plate-indian-kids).

## Easy ways to cut back

1. **Make water the default drink.** Offer plain water, and milk or buttermilk (chaas) at meals. Keep sweet drinks for rare occasions.
2. **Have fruit instead of juice.** A whole fruit is more filling and comes with fibre.
3. **Change what you keep at home.** Children eat what is available. If biscuits and sweets are not in the cupboard, there is less to negotiate.
4. **Serve treats in small portions.** Put one laddoo on a plate rather than the whole box on the table. Let sweets be part of a meal, not a reward or a bribe, which can make them seem more precious.
5. **Make familiar snacks less sweet.** Roasted chana, makhana, nuts for older children, sliced cucumber, sprouts chaat, idli with chutney or a plain curd bowl with fruit are easy snack options. Our post on [fussy eating](/blog/fussy-eating-children) has more ideas for pressure-free food habits.
6. **Check labels.** Compare grams of sugar per 100 g and choose the lower option.
7. **Do not ban sweets.** Strict bans can make sweet foods more tempting. Aim for "sometimes foods" with calm, matter-of-fact boundaries.

## Festivals and celebrations

Occasions like Diwali, birthdays and weddings naturally bring more sweets. A few days of extra treats is not a problem. You can enjoy them by letting your child choose a favourite or two, offering them after a proper meal, and returning to your usual pattern afterwards. Sleep matters too, because tired children often crave sugar. See [how much sleep children need](/blog/how-much-sleep-children-need).

## When to seek help

Sugar is rarely the whole story. Talk to your paediatrician, a dietitian or a child psychologist if:

- Your child is always hungry, extremely thirsty, urinating very often or losing weight without trying
- Your child is gaining weight quickly, or you are worried about their growth
- Your child eats very few foods and will only accept sweet or processed ones
- Mealtimes involve intense distress, hiding food or eating in secret
- Your child seems to use food to cope with sadness, boredom or anxiety

## The takeaway

Children do not need added sugar. Follow the WHO and NHS limits as a guide, watch for sweet drinks and packaged foods, choose whole fruit and water most of the time, and keep treats small, planned and guilt-free. Calm, consistent habits at home matter far more than any single sweet.
