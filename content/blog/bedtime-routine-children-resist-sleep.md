If your child asks for water, another story and "one more minute" every night, you are not alone. Bedtime resistance is very common, and the most reliable fix is a short, predictable routine that you repeat in the same order every night. The NHS advises a calm wind-down at a regular time, and this guide shows how to build one, what to say when your child calls out, and when to ask for help.

## Why children resist bedtime

Resisting sleep is rarely naughtiness. Common reasons include:

- **Not tired yet.** A late nap, a late dinner or a bedtime set too early can leave a child wide awake. The NHS notes that children should usually fall asleep within about 20 to 30 minutes of going to bed.
- **Still switched on.** Screens, rough play or a busy household in the evening keep the mind alert.
- **Wanting connection.** After a long school day, bedtime may be the first quiet time a child has with you. Asking for "one more story" can really mean "stay with me".
- **Wanting some control.** Children have little say in their day. Bedtime is one place where they can push back.
- **Worries or fears.** The dark, a tough day at school or an upcoming test can make a child want company.

## How much sleep do children need?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommends these totals over 24 hours, including naps:

| Age | Sleep needed |
| --- | --- |
| 3 to 5 years | 10 to 13 hours |
| 6 to 12 years | 9 to 12 hours |
| 13 to 18 years | 8 to 10 hours |

Work backwards from the time your child must wake up. If school starts at 8, a 7-year-old waking at 6:30 needs to be asleep by about 9 pm at the latest. For the full picture, see our guide to [how much sleep children need](/blog/how-much-sleep-children-need).

## Build a calm bedtime routine in five steps

Health services such as the NHS and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend a consistent routine because the body learns to link the same steps with sleep. Aim for about 20 to 30 minutes in total.

1. **Pick a fixed bedtime and wake-up time.** Keep weekends within about an hour of school days.
2. **Switch off screens before the routine starts.** The AAP advises turning off screens at least an hour before bed. Our guide to [screen time for children](/blog/screen-time-guidelines-children) has more.
3. **Choose three or four quiet steps.** For example: wash and brush teeth, change into night clothes, a story, lights out. Do them in the same order.
4. **Let your child choose small things.** Which book, which pyjamas, which soft toy. A little control means a little less resistance.
5. **End with a short goodnight ritual.** A hug, a phrase such as "See you in the morning", and a cuddle for the soft toy. Then leave, and keep the ritual the same every night.

A light, early dinner and a dim, cool room help too. In many homes, adults are still talking, cooking or watching TV late into the evening. Try to keep the area near the child's room quiet, or use a fan to soften household sounds.

## What to do when your child keeps calling out

Expect some testing, especially in the first week or two. Sleep services such as the Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne suggest staying calm, keeping replies short and boring, and repeating the same message: "It's sleep time. I love you. See you in the morning."

- Cover the usual requests before lights out: toilet, a sip of water, one last hug.
- Give a short, calm check-in if your child calls, rather than a long chat or a new activity.
- If your child leaves the bed, walk them back quietly and without a lecture. Be ready to do it several times for a few nights.
- Praise the next morning: "You stayed in bed last night. Well done."

Consistency matters more than any single trick. Changing the rules every night makes the resistance last longer.

## When a bedtime routine is not the whole story

If your child is anxious, a few minutes of talking about the day, or naming one good thing that happened, can help them settle. Fears of the dark or of being alone are common in the early school years; a night light and a calm, brief reassurance usually work better than long conversations at midnight.

## When to seek help

Speak to a paediatrician or a child psychologist if your child:

- Regularly takes more than 30 to 45 minutes to fall asleep despite a steady routine for several weeks
- Snores loudly most nights, gasps or seems to pause breathing in sleep
- Wakes often through the night, or has frequent nightmares or night terrors
- Is very sleepy, irritable or has trouble concentrating during the day
- Seems worried, low or very fearful at bedtime, or refuses to sleep alone for a long time
- Has sleep problems together with changes in behaviour, mood or school performance

Sleep difficulties are often linked with worry, stress or developmental differences, and they usually improve with the right support. You do not have to work it out alone.

## The takeaway

Children resist bedtime for many ordinary reasons: not being tired, wanting you close, or needing a little control. A fixed bedtime, no screens before the routine, three or four calm steps in the same order, and short, consistent responses to calling out will help in most families. Give it a couple of weeks, and if sleep is still a struggle, reach out for support.
