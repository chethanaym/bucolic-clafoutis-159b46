Children aged 5 to 17 need an average of at least 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous physical activity, according to the World Health Organization (WHO). Younger children need even more movement, spread through the day. This guide explains the WHO guidelines by age, what actually counts, and how to fit movement into a busy school-and-homework routine without turning it into another chore.

## What the WHO recommends by age

| Age | WHO guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Under 1 year | Be physically active several times a day, mainly through floor-based play. Babies who are not yet mobile should get at least 30 minutes of tummy time spread across the day while awake. |
| 1 to 4 years | At least 180 minutes a day of physical activity of any intensity, spread through the day. For 3 to 4 year olds, at least 60 of those minutes should be moderate to vigorous. More is better. |
| 5 to 17 years | An average of at least 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous, mostly aerobic activity across the week. More than 60 minutes brings extra benefit. |

For ages 5 to 17, the WHO adds that vigorous activity and activities that strengthen muscle and bone should be included at least three days a week. It also advises limiting the time children spend sitting, especially in front of screens for fun.

## What "moderate" and "vigorous" mean

You do not need a gym or a coach. A simple test:

- **Moderate activity:** your child breathes a little harder and feels warmer, but can still talk. Brisk walking, cycling to school, playing catch, dancing.
- **Vigorous activity:** breathing is heavy and talking is hard. Running games, football, skipping rope, swimming laps, fast cycling.
- **Muscle and bone strengthening:** climbing, jumping, hopscotch, carrying light loads, push-ups or yoga poses held for a few breaths.

The 60 minutes also does not have to happen in one go. Ten minutes before school, a PE class, a walk home and an evening play session all count.

## Why movement matters beyond fitness

The WHO links regular physical activity in children with better fitness, healthier weight, stronger bones and better heart health. It is also linked with better thinking skills, sleep and mood. Active children tend to sleep more soundly, which is one reason our guide to [how much sleep children need](/blog/how-much-sleep-children-need) and this one go hand in hand.

## Why many children fall short

The WHO has reported that most adolescents worldwide, around four in five, do not reach the 60-minute recommendation. In Indian cities the reasons are familiar: long school hours, tuition classes, heavy homework, traffic, limited safe playgrounds, hot weather, monsoon rain, and screens that are easier than a trip downstairs.

None of this is a parenting failure. It simply means movement has to be planned for, a little like meals and sleep.

## Easy ways to add movement to everyday life

1. **Make it social.** Children move more with other children. Evening play in the apartment complex or park, or a weekend game with cousins, is often better than a formal class.
2. **Walk or cycle where you safely can.** Even part of the school journey counts.
3. **Protect play, not just sport.** Running games, hide-and-seek, kabaddi, kho-kho and skipping all count. Children do not need to be good at it to benefit.
4. **Break up long sitting.** After 30 to 45 minutes of homework, a five-minute stretch, stairs or a quick dance break helps children return to work with more focus.
5. **Move together.** A family walk after dinner, a Sunday cycle ride or a few yoga poses at home. Children copy what adults do far more than what they are told.
6. **Plan for rain and heat.** Indoor options: dancing, skipping, a home obstacle course, yoga, stair games. In summer, go out early morning or late evening, and keep water handy.
7. **Let your child choose.** The activity a child enjoys is the one they will keep doing. Try a few before settling.
8. **Keep screens in their place.** Our guide to [screen time for children](/blog/screen-time-guidelines-children) explains how to protect time for play without daily battles.

## For children who do not enjoy sport

Some children feel awkward, slow or self-conscious in team games, and avoid them. Offer other routes to movement: swimming, dance, martial arts, trekking, gardening, cycling, skating or walking the dog. Praise effort and enjoyment, not performance. Pressure and comparison can switch a child off movement for years.

## Children with extra needs

Children with physical disabilities, developmental differences or long-term health conditions benefit from movement too, and the WHO guidelines encourage activity for them as well, adapted to their needs. Speak with your child's doctor or therapist about what is suitable and safe.

## When to seek help

Most children simply need more opportunity and encouragement. It is worth speaking to your child's doctor or a psychologist if you notice:

- Your child is very reluctant to move, tires unusually quickly, or complains of pain, dizziness or chest discomfort during activity (see a doctor promptly)
- They avoid play, sport or school because of fear, embarrassment, teasing or body image worries
- Movement has become compulsive or distressing, for example exercising in secret, or distress when they cannot exercise
- They have withdrawn from friends and things they used to enjoy, or seem low, anxious or irritable most days for a couple of weeks or more
- Changes in eating alongside any of the above

Support is available, and early advice often makes things simpler. A first conversation can help you understand what is going on and plan a gentle next step.

## The takeaway

Under-fives need plenty of movement spread through the day, and children from 5 to 17 need an average of at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity a day. It does not need to be formal, and it can be built up in small pieces. Make it fun, make it social, move with them, and keep the focus on enjoyment. A child who learns to love moving is building a habit for life.
