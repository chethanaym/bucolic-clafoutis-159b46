You do not need hours to build a close bond with your child. Short, regular stretches of full attention, even ten minutes, can help a child feel noticed and secure. With Children's Day on 14 November, it is a good moment to start a small daily habit. This guide gives you simple ideas that fit around school, work and homework.

## Why short, focused time works

Children mostly want to feel that a parent is paying attention to them. A long evening with a phone in your hand can feel less connected to a child than ten minutes of undivided attention. Mental health toolkits from the American Academy of Pediatrics describe "special time": a short daily or near-daily period of one-on-one time, commonly 10 to 15 minutes, where the child leads the play and the adult follows. The idea is to make it regular and predictable, rather than long and rare.

Regular warm time does not remove every difficulty, and it is not a treatment. But it gives children a steady base, and many parents find that everyday friction eases when children know they will get their share of attention.

## How to make ten minutes count

- **Switch off distractions.** Put your phone away, mute notifications and turn off the television. Even a few minutes without screens is enough.
- **Let your child choose.** Within reason, they pick the activity. You follow, and resist the urge to teach or correct.
- **Describe, don't direct.** Say what you see: "You're building a tall tower." Skip questions and instructions where you can.
- **Keep it regular.** Same time, most days, matters more than the length. Set a gentle alarm if you need one.
- **End kindly.** Give a warning ("Two more minutes"), then a hug or a promise: "Same time tomorrow."
- **Don't take it away as punishment.** Connection time should stay safe and dependable, even on a hard day.

## Ten-minute ideas for different ages

| Age | Ideas |
|---|---|
| 2–5 years | Building blocks, playing with clay, pretend tea party, drawing together, rolling a ball |
| 6–10 years | A card or board game, carrom, cooking a simple snack together, a short walk around the block, reading aloud |
| 11–14 years | Walking to the shop together, listening to a song they like, a quick chat over a snack, watching one short video they choose |
| 15+ years | A cup of tea or milk and a talk, a lift to class with no phones, asking their opinion on a family decision |

## Fitting it into a busy day

Many families in Bengaluru have long commutes and demanding school schedules. These small slots tend to work:

1. **Morning:** Ten minutes at breakfast, with no phones at the table.
2. **After school:** A snack and a chat before homework starts. Ask open questions like "What was the best part of your day?"
3. **Evening routine:** Turn bath, dinner or tidy-up time into shared time. Singing or storytelling counts.
4. **Bedtime:** Ten quiet minutes of reading or talking can end the day well. Good sleep also matters: see our guide on [how much sleep children need](/blog/how-much-sleep-children-need).
5. **Weekends:** A walk, a visit to the park, or cooking together.

If both parents work, or grandparents help with childcare, you can share the load. Each adult can offer their own ten minutes. Children benefit from several warm relationships.

## A few words on guilt

Most parents feel they are not doing enough. Some days will be rushed, and that is fine. Children do not need a perfect parent, only a warm and reasonably consistent one. If you miss a day, simply pick up again the next one, and tell your child you enjoyed being with them.

## Screens and connection

Phones are often what steals attention. The World Health Organization advises limiting sedentary screen time in young children and replacing it with active play and interaction. Our post on [screen time guidelines by age](/blog/screen-time-guidelines-children) explains the limits. A simple family rule such as "no phones during meals" creates easy connection time without extra effort.

## What if my child resists?

Some children, especially older ones, may say "I'm fine" or seem uninterested at first. That is normal. Keep offering without pressure, choose something they already like, and let silence be comfortable. Trust usually builds slowly, through many small, ordinary moments.

If your child has recently had a big change, such as a new school, a family move or a loss, they may need extra patience and more frequent connection.

## When to seek help

Closeness is a strong foundation, but it cannot solve every difficulty. Consider speaking to a child psychologist, counsellor or your paediatrician if your child:

- Seems sad, withdrawn or irritable for more than two weeks
- Has worries or fears that interfere with school, sleep or friendships
- Has frequent, intense anger or meltdowns that are hard to settle
- Avoids school or things they used to enjoy
- Has sudden changes in sleep, appetite or behaviour
- Talks about hurting themselves or not wanting to be alive (seek help the same day)

You may also want support if *you* feel constantly overwhelmed, since a parent's wellbeing affects the whole family. Asking for help is a sign of care, and our team is happy to talk through what would suit your family.

## The takeaway

Connection is built in small moments. Ten minutes of device-free, child-led time, most days, is a realistic habit for busy families. Start small, be consistent, follow your child's lead and be kind to yourself on days it does not happen. If concerns grow, reach out for support early.
